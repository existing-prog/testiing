1. Research overview

Trans-Neptunian objects preserve information about the formation and evolution of the outer Solar System. This research journal develops the mathematical and computational framework used to study the structures that organize their motion, with particular emphasis on the circular restricted three-body problem (CR3BP), zero-velocity geometry, periodic orbits, and transport near Neptune.

The presentation is intentionally cumulative. Each model begins with its assumptions and reference frames, proceeds through the governing equations and nondimensionalization, and ends with numerical implementation and validation. This makes the derivations auditable and creates a direct path from the mathematics to the interactive tools and research code.

Journal status. This is a working research journal. The CR3BP foundations below have been developed from earlier course notes and rewritten for this project; later chapters will add references, reproducible figures, periodic-orbit families, invariant manifolds, and results specific to trans-Neptunian dynamics.

2. Circular restricted three-body model

2.1 Bodies and assumptions

Consider two finite masses, m1m_1m1​ and m2m_2m2​, called the primaries, and a third body of mass m3m_3m3​. The restricted model is obtained by requiring m3≪m2≤m1m_3 \ll m_2 \leq m_1m3​≪m2​≤m1​, so the third body responds to the gravity of the primaries but does not measurably alter their motion. This approximation is appropriate for a spacecraft, comet, or trans-Neptunian object moving in the gravitational field of the Sun and Neptune.

The circular restricted three-body problem makes the following assumptions:

  1. Each primary is a point mass, or equivalently has a spherically symmetric exterior gravity field;
  2. The third body is massless in the dynamical sense described above;
  3. The primaries follow circular Keplerian orbits about their common barycenter;
  4. The separation between the primaries and their angular speed are constant;
  5. No forces other than the gravity of the two primaries are included.

The first two assumptions do more than declare that the third body is small. They decouple the problem. In the general three-body problem, the motion of every body changes the gravitational field seen by the other two, so all three trajectories must be solved simultaneously. In the restricted problem, the two primaries form a closed two-body subsystem whose motion is known in advance. Their positions can therefore be treated as prescribed inputs to the differential equation for the third body.

The circular assumption supplies the next major simplification. A Keplerian ellipse has a changing separation and angular rate, so a frame attached to the line between the primaries would rotate nonuniformly and change scale throughout each orbit. Circular motion keeps both quantities constant. The rotating equations then contain no explicit time dependence, which makes an energy-like first integral possible and turns stationary points in the rotating frame into meaningful dynamical landmarks.

The primaries therefore remain in a fixed plane, but the third body may move in all three spatial dimensions. The model is autonomous in a frame rotating with the primaries.

The planarity of the primaries follows from conservation of angular momentum in their two-body motion. It does not require the third body to remain in that plane. Setting z=0z=0z=0 produces the planar CR3BP as a special case; retaining zzz allows halo orbits, vertical oscillations, and other spatial motion.

These assumptions are useful because they isolate the geometry generated by a single primary pair. They also define the model's limits. Planetary eccentricity, inclination, perturbations from additional planets, radiation forces, and the finite mass of the third body must be introduced separately when those effects become dynamically important.

4. Effective potential and the Jacobi integral

4.1 Effective potential

The component equations in Eq. (3.2) contain three kinds of terms: gravitational acceleration from each primary, centrifugal acceleration caused by the rotating coordinates, and Coriolis acceleration caused by motion within those coordinates. The first two depend only on position. It is useful to collect them into one scalar function whose spatial derivatives reproduce every position-dependent term in the equations of motion.

To construct that function, first note that differentiating (1−μ)/r1(1-\mu)/r_1(1−μ)/r1​ or μ/r2\mu/r_2μ/r2​ produces the corresponding gravitational acceleration toward a primary. For example,

∂∂x(1−μr1)=−(1−μ)(x+μ)r13. \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{1-\mu}{r_1}\right) =-\frac{(1-\mu)(x+\mu)}{r_1^3}. ∂x∂​(r1​1−μ​)=−r13​(1−μ)(x+μ)​.

Similarly, differentiating (x2+y2)/2(x^2+y^2)/2(x2+y2)/2 produces (x,y,0)(x,y,0)(x,y,0), the nondimensional centrifugal acceleration. There is no z2/2z^2/2z2/2 contribution because the frame rotates about the zzz-axis: centrifugal acceleration depends on distance from that axis, x2+y2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}x2+y2​, and has no zzz-component.

These observations motivate the definition of the rotating-frame effective potential

Here, subscripts denote partial derivatives. The word effective emphasizes that Ω\OmegaΩ combines the gravitational and centrifugal contributions seen in the rotating frame. 4.2 Derivation of the integral

Because the rotating-frame equations contain no explicit time dependence, it is reasonable to search for a quantity that remains constant along a trajectory. The useful step is to convert acceleration into the time derivative of squared speed. To do this, multiply the three equations in Eq. (4.2) by their corresponding velocity components, x˙\dot{x}x˙, y˙\dot{y}y˙​, and z˙\dot{z}z˙, and add:

x˙(x¨−2y˙)+y˙(y¨+2x˙)+z˙z¨=Ωxx˙+Ωyy˙+Ωzz˙. \dot{x}(\ddot{x}-2\dot{y}) +\dot{y}(\ddot{y}+2\dot{x}) +\dot{z}\ddot{z} =\Omega_x\dot{x}+\Omega_y\dot{y}+\Omega_z\dot{z}. x˙(x¨−2y˙​)+y˙​(y¨​+2x˙)+z˙z¨=Ωx​x˙+Ωy​y˙​+Ωz​z˙.

The Coriolis contributions cancel algebraically,

−2x˙y˙+2y˙x˙=0, -2\dot{x}\dot{y}+2\dot{y}\dot{x}=0, −2x˙y˙​+2y˙​x˙=0,

leaving

x˙x¨+y˙y¨+z˙z¨=Ωxx˙+Ωyy˙+Ωzz˙. \dot{x}\ddot{x}+\dot{y}\ddot{y}+\dot{z}\ddot{z} =\Omega_x\dot{x}+\Omega_y\dot{y}+\Omega_z\dot{z}. x˙x¨+y˙​y¨​+z˙z¨=Ωx​x˙+Ωy​y˙​+Ωz​z˙.

This cancellation is also physically expected. Coriolis acceleration is perpendicular to the rotating-frame velocity, so it can redirect the motion but cannot directly change the speed or kinetic energy. This is the meaning of the statement that the Coriolis force “does no work.”

The remaining terms are total derivatives: the left side is the derivative of one-half the squared speed, and the right side is the derivative of Ω\OmegaΩ along the trajectory. Thus

ddτ[12(x˙2+y˙2+z˙2)]=dΩdτ⟹ddτ[2Ω−(x˙2+y˙2+z˙2)]=0. \frac{d}{d\tau} \left[\frac{1}{2}(\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2+\dot{z}^2)\right] =\frac{d\Omega}{d\tau} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{d\tau} \left[2\Omega-(\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2+\dot{z}^2)\right]=0. dτd​[21​(x˙2+y˙​2+z˙2)]=dτdΩ​⟹dτd​[2Ω−(x˙2+y˙​2+z˙2)]=0.

The expression in the final brackets is therefore constant along an unforced CR3BP trajectory. By convention, it is called the Jacobi constant:

C=2Ω(x,y,z)−(x˙2+y˙2+z˙2).(4.3) C=2\Omega(x,y,z) -\left(\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2+\dot{z}^2\right). \tag{4.3} C=2Ω(x,y,z)−(x˙2+y˙​2+z˙2).(4.3)

The CR3BP is autonomous in the rotating frame, and CCC is its conserved first integral. Rearranging Eq. (4.3) makes its practical role clear:

x˙2+y˙2+z˙2=2Ω−C. \dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2+\dot{z}^2=2\Omega-C. x˙2+y˙​2+z˙2=2Ω−C.

For a specified value of CCC, position determines the speed that would be required there. If 2Ω−C<02\Omega-C<02Ω−C<0, the required squared speed would be negative, so that position is inaccessible to the trajectory. If 2Ω−C=02\Omega-C=02Ω−C=0, the rotating-frame speed is zero and the position lies on a zero-velocity boundary. This is why deriving the Jacobi integral is useful: it converts a conserved quantity into a direct geometric restriction on motion without requiring the trajectory to be integrated first.

The Jacobi constant is related to the rotating-frame Hamiltonian by C=−2HC=-2HC=−2H, so increasing CCC corresponds to decreasing rotating-frame energy. It is not the ordinary inertial mechanical energy. It combines rotating-frame kinetic energy with the gravitational and centrifugal contributions collected in Ω\OmegaΩ. Calling it an energy-like integral is useful, but treating it as inertial energy would obscure the role of the rotating coordinates.

5. Equilibrium points and accessible geometry

5.1 Equilibrium condition

An equilibrium point is stationary in the rotating frame, so its velocity and acceleration are both zero. Equation (4.2) then requires

∇Ω(x,y,z)=0.(5.1) \nabla\Omega(x,y,z)=\mathbf 0. \tag{5.1} ∇Ω(x,y,z)=0.(5.1)

“Stationary” is frame dependent. A body at a libration point is not motionless in inertial space; it revolves with the primary pair at angular rate nnn. Its gravitational and centrifugal contributions balance in the rotating frame, and its zero rotating-frame velocity eliminates the Coriolis term.

The zzz-equation implies z=0z=0z=0, so all five classical equilibrium points lie in the orbital plane. Three are collinear with the primaries and two form equilateral triangles with them.

For the collinear points, y=z=0y=z=0y=z=0, and the equilibrium condition reduces to a scalar equation containing absolute values. It is convenient to solve for the positive distance γi\gamma_iγi​ from the nearest primary. Define

The roots are computed iteratively with Newton's method,

γi(k+1)=γi(k)−fi(γi(k))fi′(γi(k)). \gamma_i^{(k+1)} =\gamma_i^{(k)} -\frac{f_i(\gamma_i^{(k)})}{f_i'(\gamma_i^{(k)})}. γi(k+1)​=γi(k)​−fi′​(γi(k)​)fi​(γi(k)​)​.

5.6 Planar zero-velocity curves and forbidden regions

The planar CR3BP is obtained by imposing z=z˙=0z=\dot z=0z=z˙=0. With those restrictions, the Jacobi integral in Eq. (4.3) can be solved for the in-plane speed:

ΓC={(x,y):2Ω(x,y,0)=C}.(5.4) \Gamma_C =\left\{(x,y):2\Omega(x,y,0)=C\right\}. \tag{5.4} ΓC​={(x,y):2Ω(x,y,0)=C}.(5.4)

5.7 Zero-velocity surfaces

The same argument extends to spatial motion. Restoring zzz and all three velocity components gives

6. Numerical propagation and verification

6.1 First-order state model

Numerical integrators operate on a first-order system. Define

X=[xyzx˙y˙z˙] ⁣T. \mathbf X= \begin{bmatrix} x&y&z&\dot{x}&\dot{y}&\dot{z} \end{bmatrix}^{\!T}. X=[x​y​z​x˙​y˙​​z˙​]T.

A second-order vector equation is rewritten this way because general-purpose integration algorithms advance a state from its current value and first derivative. Position and velocity are therefore carried together. This also makes event detection, variational equations, and later state-transition-matrix calculations compatible with the same state representation.

Then

X˙=[x˙y˙z˙2y˙+Ωx−2x˙+ΩyΩz].(6.1) \dot{\mathbf X}= \begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}\\ \dot{y}\\ \dot{z}\\ 2\dot{y}+\Omega_x\\ -2\dot{x}+\Omega_y\\ \Omega_z \end{bmatrix}. \tag{6.1} X˙=​x˙y˙​z˙2y˙​+Ωx​−2x˙+Ωy​Ωz​​​.(6.1)

This expression is the common interface between the analytical model and an adaptive numerical integrator.

6.2 Dimensional reconstruction

Nondimensional results are converted back to physical units with Eq. (3.1). For a primary system with separation aaa, total gravitational parameter G(m1+m2)G(m_1+m_2)G(m1​+m2​), and state (r,v)(\mathbf r,\mathbf v)(r,v),

r~=ar,v~=G(m1+m2)a v. \widetilde{\mathbf r}=a\mathbf r, \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf v}=\sqrt{\frac{G(m_1+m_2)}{a}}\,\mathbf v. r=ar,v=aG(m1​+m2​)​​v.

Reporting both normalized and dimensional values prevents scale conversions from becoming hidden implementation details.

6.3 Jacobi conservation as a diagnostic

For an unforced CR3BP trajectory, the computed Jacobi constant should remain equal to its initial value. Define the drift

ΔC(τ)=C(X(τ))−C(X(0)). \Delta C(\tau)=C(\mathbf X(\tau))-C(\mathbf X(0)). ΔC(τ)=C(X(τ))−C(X(0)).

The analytical model conserves CCC exactly, whereas a numerical solver accumulates truncation and roundoff error. Monitoring the invariant supplies a physics-based diagnostic that is more informative than relying on the solver's internal success flag. A solver can finish successfully and still produce a trajectory whose invariant has drifted enough to cross a narrow dynamical gateway artificially.

The maximum and root-mean-square values of ΔC\Delta CΔC are recorded for every propagation. A small Jacobi drift is necessary but not sufficient for an accurate trajectory: long integrations near separatrices can remain highly sensitive to small changes in tolerances, initial conditions, and arithmetic precision.

A defensible convergence study therefore repeats the propagation with progressively tighter relative and absolute tolerances and compares:

  • The complete state history over a shared time interval;
  • Event times such as crossings of a chosen surface;
  • The maximum Jacobi drift;
  • The result under a reduced maximum step size; and
  • Agreement between independent integration methods when practical.

The tightest tolerance is not automatically the best scientific choice. A tolerance is accepted when the quantities used in the analysis have converged while the computational cost remains reasonable.

The “bouncing” appearance seen when such trajectories approach a zero-velocity boundary should be interpreted carefully. No collision occurs with the curve. 6.5 Verification checklist

Before a trajectory or surface is used in the research, the implementation is checked against the following conditions:

  1. The primary positions satisfy the barycenter relation;
  2. The computed equilibrium points make ∥∇Ω∥\lVert\nabla\Omega\rVert∥∇Ω∥ numerically zero;
  3. The Jacobi constant at each equilibrium matches the reported critical value;
  4. The zero-velocity boundary satisfies 2Ω−C=02\Omega-C=02Ω−C=0 to the extraction tolerance;
  5. Dimensional and nondimensional states round-trip through the unit conversion;
  6. Jacobi drift and state histories converge as integration tolerances are tightened; and
  7. Coordinate orientation and sign conventions agree across equations, code, and figures.

7. Role in trans-Neptunian dynamics

For the Sun-Neptune pair, the CR3BP provides a geometric baseline for studying motion near Neptune's orbit. Its equilibria, periodic orbits, and invariant manifolds organize pathways through phase space and supply a language for discussing temporary capture, resonance transitions, and transport between dynamically distinct regions.

The model is not a complete representation of the outer Solar System. Neptune's eccentricity, its inclination relative to a chosen reference plane, the gravity of the other giant planets, and resonant forcing can all modify the motion. The research strategy is therefore hierarchical: first identify mechanisms in the autonomous Sun-Neptune CR3BP, then test which structures persist as additional physical effects are introduced.

Planned extensions of this chapter include:
